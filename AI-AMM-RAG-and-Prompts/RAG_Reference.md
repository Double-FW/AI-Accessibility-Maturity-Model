# Introduction

Accessibility maturity models were originally designed to help organisations understand how effectively accessibility was embedded into governance, culture, delivery, procurement, and operational practice. Traditionally, these models focused heavily on websites, applications, policy frameworks, training, and compliance processes.

Those foundations still matter. However, organisations are now operating inside environments that are increasingly shaped by artificial intelligence, automation, predictive systems, machine-generated content, algorithmic decision-making, and continuously evolving digital ecosystems.

This changes the role of accessibility maturity significantly.

Accessibility can no longer be treated as a static technical requirement applied at the end of delivery. AI-enabled organisations now create experiences dynamically through systems that continuously learn, adapt, prioritise, and automate decisions at scale.

In this environment, accessibility maturity becomes a measure of whether an organisation is capable of producing inclusive outcomes consistently across:
- customer experiences
- employee experiences
- operational systems
- AI-assisted workflows
- procurement ecosystems
- governance structures
- decision-making systems
- automated services

An AI-ready accessibility maturity model must therefore evaluate far more than technical compliance. It must assess whether inclusion is operationally embedded into how the organisation designs systems, governs risk, manages data, deploys AI, measures outcomes, and defines quality itself.

Most importantly, the model must evaluate whether disabled people experience comparable confidence, autonomy, dignity, participation, and trust across systems that are increasingly automated and adaptive.

---

# Why accessibility maturity must evolve for AI-enabled organisations

Traditional accessibility maturity models were largely designed for stable digital environments. Organisations assessed accessibility through audits, procurement reviews, accessibility statements, and periodic remediation exercises.

AI changes both the speed and scale at which exclusion can occur.

An inaccessible webpage may create a barrier within a single interaction. An inaccessible AI-enabled operational system may influence thousands or millions of interactions simultaneously across recruitment, support services, internal operations, communications, customer journeys, and decision-making systems.

The nature of accessibility risk therefore changes fundamentally.

For example, a recruitment platform may technically conform to accessibility standards while still disadvantaging neurodivergent applicants through behavioural scoring models. A customer support chatbot may function correctly while failing users with cognitive disabilities because it cannot adapt to different communication styles. Predictive systems may deprioritise accommodations because operational efficiency metrics were prioritised more strongly than inclusion outcomes.

These forms of exclusion are often difficult to detect through traditional accessibility testing because they emerge through:
- operational assumptions
- governance failures
- inaccessible datasets
- procurement decisions
- automation logic
- organisational incentives
- behavioural modelling

Accessibility maturity must therefore evolve from a compliance-centred framework into an operational inclusion framework capable of governing continuously changing systems.

# Core principles of the model

## Accessibility is organisational infrastructure

Accessibility should not be treated as a standalone initiative or specialist activity. It is infrastructure that influences service quality, operational quality, customer trust, employee participation, governance credibility, and organisational resilience.

Low-maturity organisations treat accessibility as remediation. Mature organisations treat accessibility as part of how systems are designed and governed.

---

## Comparable experience is the primary outcome

The purpose of accessibility maturity is not merely technical conformance.

The purpose is to ensure disabled people can participate with comparable:
- dignity
- confidence
- autonomy
- efficiency
- trust
- opportunity

Comparable does not mean identical. Different users may complete tasks differently. What matters is whether the quality of the outcome remains equitable.

---

## AI systems are part of accessibility governance

AI governance and accessibility governance can no longer operate separately.

AI systems increasingly influence:
- recruitment
- customer support
- employee systems
- communications
- service delivery
- content generation
- operational prioritisation
- decision-making

An organisation cannot claim accessibility maturity if its AI systems produce exclusionary outcomes.

---

## Accessibility maturity is continuous

Accessibility maturity is not a final state.

Technology, operational systems, AI models, and customer expectations will continue evolving. Mature organisations therefore develop the capability to continuously learn, adapt, govern, monitor, and improve inclusion over time.

---

# The structure of the maturity model

The AI-Ready Accessibility Maturity Model evaluates organisations across six connected dimensions:

1. Leadership and Governance  
2. Culture and Capability  
3. Design, Delivery, and Operations  
4. Data, Measurement, and Comparable Experience  
5. AI, Automation, and Intelligent Systems  
6. Ethics, Risk, and Organisational Trust  

These dimensions are evaluated across five maturity levels:

1. Reactive  
2. Foundational  
3. Operational  
4. Integrated  
5. Transformative  

The model is designed to assess both organisational capability and lived experience outcomes.

---

# The six maturity dimensions

## 1. Leadership and Governance

This dimension evaluates whether accessibility has visible authority, operational accountability, and strategic influence across the organisation.

In low-maturity organisations, accessibility leadership is fragmented, informal, or dependent on individual advocacy. Accessibility may exist within policies but lack influence over procurement, operational strategy, AI adoption, or investment decisions.

As maturity develops, accessibility becomes embedded into mainstream governance structures. Executive sponsors actively govern inclusion outcomes rather than simply approving accessibility statements. Accessibility risks are escalated formally alongside operational, security, and regulatory risks.

At higher maturity levels, governance structures increasingly include:
- accessibility steering groups
- AI ethics councils
- procurement accountability
- operational KPIs
- roadmap ownership
- inclusion reporting mechanisms
- escalation pathways
- executive accountability

Transformative organisations treat accessibility as a core governance capability that influences organisational strategy, operational quality, and long-term trust.

---

## 2. Culture and Capability

This dimension evaluates whether inclusion is embedded into organisational behaviour, operational thinking, and workforce capability.

Low-maturity organisations often position accessibility as a technical or compliance issue owned by specialists. Teams may have limited understanding of disability inclusion, inclusive design, or accessible operational practice.

Mature organisations distribute accessibility capability across:
- product teams
- design teams
- procurement teams
- engineering
- HR
- customer support
- operational leadership
- AI governance functions

Accessibility becomes part of how quality is understood throughout the organisation.

Capability development evolves beyond WCAG knowledge alone. Teams increasingly require understanding of:
- inclusive service design
- ethical AI
- operational bias
- accessible communications
- assistive technologies
- human-centred automation
- inclusive research practices
- comparable experience evaluation

At the highest maturity levels, inclusion becomes culturally embedded. Accessibility is considered naturally during operational planning, procurement, innovation, AI adoption, and organisational transformation.

---

## 3. Design, Delivery, and Operations

This dimension evaluates whether accessibility is embedded throughout organisational workflows and operational systems.

Low-maturity organisations treat accessibility reactively. Barriers are identified late and remediated after deployment.

Mature organisations shift accessibility left across:
- strategy
- procurement
- architecture
- design systems
- content operations
- development workflows
- QA processes
- operational tooling
- release management
- AI deployment pipelines

Accessibility becomes preventative rather than corrective.

Operational maturity also includes internal systems. Mature organisations recognise that inaccessible employee tooling, operational platforms, and workplace systems create exclusion just as significantly as inaccessible customer services.

At higher maturity levels, accessibility becomes integrated into continuous delivery environments. Automated monitoring, regression detection, operational governance, and inclusive design systems work together to reduce accessibility debt before it scales.

---

## 4. Data, Measurement, and Comparable Experience

This dimension evaluates whether organisations can measure inclusion meaningfully and ethically.

Low-maturity organisations often rely heavily on technical compliance metrics while collecting little meaningful insight about lived experience.

Mature organisations recognise that users do not experience compliance. They experience journeys.

As maturity develops, organisations increasingly measure:
- disabled customer satisfaction
- disabled employee experience
- support dependency
- friction patterns
- abandonment trends
- operational barriers
- confidence levels
- journey completion quality
- emotional effort
- trust indicators

Importantly, mature organisations recognise that disability-related data is highly sensitive.

Measurement practices therefore evolve towards:
- consent-based insight gathering
- ethical data governance
- representative research participation
- transparent analytics practices
- privacy-aware operational monitoring

At higher maturity levels, organisations develop the capability to identify exclusion patterns proactively rather than waiting for complaints or escalation.

---

## 5. AI, Automation, and Intelligent Systems

This dimension evaluates whether accessibility and inclusion are embedded into AI-enabled operational environments.

Low-maturity organisations may deploy AI systems without understanding their accessibility implications. AI adoption may occur informally without governance, auditing, or inclusion oversight.

Mature organisations integrate accessibility into:
- AI procurement
- AI governance
- model evaluation
- conversational systems
- predictive systems
- operational automation
- content generation
- intelligent workflows

Accessibility considerations increasingly include:
- representational fairness
- accessible conversational design
- human override capability
- operational transparency
- AI auditing
- accessible automation pathways
- disability-aware testing
- inclusive training datasets

At higher maturity levels, organisations use AI itself to improve accessibility through:
- predictive monitoring
- automated captioning
- content adaptation
- accessibility pattern detection
- assistive interaction support
- personalised accessibility features

However, mature organisations also recognise that AI-generated accessibility solutions require human oversight and continuous evaluation.

---

## 6. Ethics, Risk, and Organisational Trust

This dimension evaluates whether organisations can govern inclusion responsibly across complex systems.

Low-maturity organisations often focus narrowly on legal compliance while overlooking broader ethical risks associated with exclusion, bias, automation, and operational harm.

Mature organisations increasingly recognise that accessibility failures create:
- governance risks
- trust risks
- reputational risks
- operational risks
- employee risks
- customer experience risks
- AI ethics risks

As maturity develops, organisations establish:
- ethical review mechanisms
- operational risk evaluation
- inclusion impact assessment
- escalation procedures
- transparency requirements
- accountability frameworks
- independent review capability

At the highest maturity levels, accessibility becomes part of organisational trust architecture. Inclusion is treated as fundamental to governance credibility, customer confidence, and long-term organisational legitimacy.

---

# The five maturity levels

## Level 1 — Reactive

Accessibility activity is inconsistent and largely reactive. Work focuses on remediation after complaints, audits, or legal concerns emerge.

Accessibility depends heavily on individuals rather than operational systems.

AI systems may already be influencing organisational decisions without accessibility oversight.

---

## Level 2 — Foundational

Basic governance structures, policies, and accessibility processes begin to emerge.

Accessibility is increasingly recognised as important, but operational integration remains inconsistent.

AI adoption may include limited accessibility consideration, though governance maturity remains immature.

---

## Level 3 — Operational

Accessibility becomes embedded into delivery workflows, governance structures, procurement, and operational planning.

Accessibility and AI governance begin interacting meaningfully.

Measurement practices evolve beyond technical compliance towards experience quality and operational insight.

---

## Level 4 — Integrated

Accessibility becomes integrated into organisational strategy, operational systems, AI governance, and continuous delivery environments.

Governance structures actively monitor inclusion outcomes.

Accessibility increasingly influences product quality, operational quality, customer experience, and employee experience simultaneously.

---

## Level 5 — Transformative

Accessibility becomes part of organisational identity and operational intelligence.

Disabled people participate directly within governance, strategic planning, AI evaluation, and organisational decision-making.

AI systems actively support participation, predict barriers, and improve inclusion outcomes responsibly.

The organisation continuously learns, adapts, measures, governs, and improves inclusion over time.

---

# How the model should be applied

The model should not be used purely as a scoring exercise.

Its purpose is to:
- identify operational strengths and weaknesses
- reveal governance gaps
- evaluate organisational alignment
- assess AI readiness
- improve comparable experience
- guide long-term transformation

Assessments should combine:
- governance review
- operational evidence
- stakeholder workshops
- journey analysis
- employee insight
- customer feedback
- AI system evaluation
- accessibility testing
- cultural assessment

The model should be reviewed periodically because organisational maturity changes continuously as systems evolve.

---

# Measuring maturity through evidence and lived experience

Mature accessibility programmes measure both operational capability and human experience.

Technical conformance remains important, but it should be balanced with:
- customer satisfaction
- employee confidence
- operational friction analysis
- support patterns
- complaint trends
- inclusion indicators
- AI outcome evaluation
- governance evidence

The strongest maturity models combine quantitative operational data with qualitative lived experience insight.

---

# Governance expectations

An AI-ready accessibility maturity model requires governance structures capable of sustaining inclusion over time.

These structures typically include:
- executive sponsorship
- accessibility steering groups
- AI ethics governance
- procurement accountability
- roadmap ownership
- escalation pathways
- operational KPIs
- periodic reassessment cycles

Governance transforms accessibility from good intention into sustainable operational capability.

---

# What transformative maturity looks like

Transformative maturity is not simply a higher level of compliance.

It represents a shift in how organisations understand inclusion, governance, quality, and operational trust.

At this level:
- accessibility influences organisational strategy
- inclusion shapes AI governance
- governance becomes proactive rather than reactive
- operational systems support participation
- AI systems help identify barriers before harm occurs
- disabled people co-lead organisational improvement

Most importantly, transformative organisations recognise that accessibility maturity is continuous.

True maturity is the ability to continuously adapt inclusion alongside technological and organisational change.

---

# In short

An AI-ready accessibility maturity model must evaluate far more than technical compliance.

It must determine whether inclusion is genuinely embedded into governance, culture, operations, procurement, AI systems, customer experience, employee experience, and organisational decision-making.

As organisations increasingly rely on AI-assisted systems, accessibility maturity becomes inseparable from governance quality, organisational trust, operational resilience, and ESG credibility.

The organisations that succeed will not simply be the ones that comply with accessibility standards. They will be the ones capable of continuously designing systems, services, operational models, and AI-enabled experiences that allow disabled people to participate with comparable confidence, dignity, autonomy, and opportunity at scale.

# AI-Ready Accessibility Maturity Model

A framework for embedding accessibility, inclusion, governance, and comparable human experience into AI-enabled organisations.

This repository presents an alternative to compliance-led accessibility maturity models (AMMs). It is designed to help organisations assess whether inclusion is genuinely operationalised across governance, culture, service design, AI systems, and lived experience, rather than treating accessibility as a narrow technical checklist.

## Why this repository exists

Traditional accessibility maturity models were largely built for relatively stable digital environments. They often centred on audits, remediation, compliance statements, and isolated process improvements. Those foundations still matter, but AI-enabled organisations now operate in contexts shaped by automation, predictive systems, intelligent workflows, and continuously changing digital services.

In this environment, exclusion can emerge not only from inaccessible interfaces, but also from:

- governance gaps
- procurement decisions
- operational assumptions
- automated decision-making
- weak accountability structures
- inaccessible data and workflows
- exclusionary design patterns
- AI systems deployed without meaningful inclusion oversight

This model reframes accessibility maturity as an organisational capability: the ability to produce inclusive outcomes consistently across customer experience, employee experience, service delivery, AI systems, operational decision-making, and governance.

## Repository contents

- `AI AMM.md` — the primary framework, narrative, dimensions, maturity levels, and governance expectations
- `README.md` — repository overview and orientation
- `LICENSE` — MIT license terms

## Core principles

The model is built around the following principles:

- Accessibility is organisational infrastructure, not a standalone specialist activity.
- Comparable experience is the primary outcome, not simply technical compliance.
- AI systems are part of accessibility governance.
- Accessibility maturity is continuous and must evolve with technology and organisational change.
- Inclusion should be evaluated through both operational evidence and lived experience.

## The six maturity dimensions

The model assesses organisations across six connected dimensions:

1. Leadership and Governance
2. Culture and Capability
3. Design, Delivery, and Operations
4. Data, Measurement, and Comparable Experience
5. AI, Automation, and Intelligent Systems
6. Ethics, Risk, and Organisational Trust

## The five maturity levels

The framework evaluates maturity across five levels:

1. Reactive
2. Foundational
3. Operational
4. Integrated
5. Transformative

## What the model aims to assess

The framework is intended to help organisations assess whether inclusion is genuinely embedded into:

- governance and leadership
- culture and workforce capability
- service design and operational processes
- procurement and delivery workflows
- AI and automation practices
- data collection and measurement
- decision-making and trust frameworks

It is designed to evaluate both organisational capability and the lived experience of disabled people.

## How to use the model

The model should be used as a strategic assessment tool rather than a simple scoring exercise. It is intended to:

- identify strengths and gaps
- expose governance weaknesses
- assess AI readiness
- evaluate operational alignment
- improve comparable experience
- guide long-term transformation

Assessments should combine evidence from governance review, operational practice, stakeholder workshops, employee and customer insight, journey analysis, AI system evaluation, accessibility testing, and cultural assessment.

## Measuring maturity

A mature accessibility programme does not only measure technical compliance. It also evaluates:

- customer satisfaction
- employee confidence
- support burden and friction
- complaint trends
- abandonment patterns
- inclusion indicators
- AI outcome quality
- governance evidence

This reflects the model's emphasis on lived experience, equitable outcomes, and ethical data practice.

## Scope and intent

This repository is document-led and focuses on strategic and governance frameworks rather than software implementation or tooling.

The framework is intended to support organisations moving beyond compliance-led thinking and building more resilient, inclusive, and AI-ready accessibility practices. The goal is not simply to meet standards, but to create systems, services, and operating models that consistently deliver fair, dignified, and trustworthy experiences for disabled people.
